## About

Eyebrow: About
H1: Same team as Clemson CubeSat, with a wider job.
Lead: We're students who want to build real spacecraft, and we think South Carolina should be one of the places where that happens.

Story
H2: Why "Orbital"
P1: Clemson CubeSat gave us the foundation: people who know how to design a small satellite, integrate it, and fly it.
P2: A mission keeps going after the spacecraft leaves the rocket. There are ground passes to schedule, data to clean up, researchers waiting on results, and a new class of students who'll inherit the next build. We picked a name that covers all of that.
P3: So we widened the scope. We still build satellites; we also plan larger missions, open our hardware to research, and speak up for aerospace in the Southeast, where plenty of good engineers currently leave to find this work.

Values
H2: How we work
- Build it to fly. A design isn't done until it survives vibration, thermal cycling, and the people who have to operate it. We test early and write down what broke.
- Write it down. Reviews are open, documents stay current, and every lesson gets handed to the next team. If only one person understands a subsystem, it isn't finished.
- Keep the South in the room. Decisions about aerospace get made in a handful of places. We want students and companies from this region in those conversations, and we want to show the work can happen here.

Who can join
H2: You don't need to know orbital mechanics yet.
P1: Most members are undergraduates in engineering, computing, and the sciences. Plenty joined without any space experience and learned it next to someone a semester ahead of them.
P2: We also work with faculty, grad students, local companies, and community groups. If you can help with a design review, a ground station, a school visit, or a funding conversation, we'd like to hear from you.

## Missions

Eyebrow: Missions
H1: Every mission starts on a whiteboard and ends in orbit.
Lead: A mission is the whole life of a spacecraft: the question it answers, the hardware that answers it, and the operations that keep it useful after launch.

Lifecycle
H2: Six phases, and each one ends in a review.
P: We don't start a phase until the last one has a written record. Members and outside advisors can sit in on any review.
01 Define: Set the goal, the requirements, and what success looks like in plain terms.
02 Design: Size the spacecraft, pick the subsystems, and check every interface before anyone cuts metal.
03 Build: Fabricate, wire, and program, keeping drawings and the parts list current.
04 Test: Vibration, thermal vacuum, and functional checks. Better to find failures on the bench.
05 Launch: Prepare for integration with a launch provider and support the spacecraft through deployment.
06 Operate: Command it, pull down the data, and get results to whoever asked for them.

Programs
H2: Where we're headed
P: Details firm up as each mission passes review. "In development" means a plan, not a launch date.
- [Flying] Small satellite missions: CubeSat-class spacecraft carrying student payloads and short research experiments. Status: details coming soon
- [In development] Larger platform: A bigger spacecraft that can carry several research payloads and run for longer. Status: concept and requirements
- [Planned] Ground station network: Partner sites around the state so we can hear the spacecraft on more passes. Status: planning

## Research

Eyebrow: Research
H1: A lab that has to survive a rocket launch.
Lead: Orbit gives researchers conditions that are hard to fake on the ground. We help get experiments there, run them safely, and bring the data home.

How it works
H2: You bring the question. We make it flyable.
P1: Researchers tell us what they need to measure. We handle the engineering around it: mechanical fit, power budget, data handling, and the safety reviews launch providers require.
P2: Students on a payload team see an experiment through every stage, and faculty get a flight platform they couldn't justify building alone.

Areas
H2: Where we focus
01 Space environment: Radiation, thermal swings, and drag, measured in place to check the models engineers rely on.
02 Small satellite systems: Attitude control, power, and software that has to run on very little hardware.
03 Sensing and imaging: Payloads that look at Earth or near space, with an eye on data regional partners can use.
04 Communications: Link budgets and ground stations for a spacecraft that's only overhead for a few minutes at a time.

Partner
H2: Bring us a question worth testing.
- Faculty and grad researchers: co-develop a payload and get the data your lab needs.
- Undergraduate teams: join a project already underway, or pitch one that fits the platform.
- Industry and agencies: sponsor a payload, donate components, or test hardware with us.

## Advocacy

Eyebrow: Advocacy
H1: Space work shouldn't require leaving the Southeast.
Lead: South Carolina has universities, manufacturers, and students who could build the next generation of space hardware. We connect them and make the case for investing here.

Position
H2: Why this region, and why now
P1: The space industry is spreading beyond its old hubs. Companies want engineers who can work on a real system from day one, and students need to see that those jobs can exist close to home.
P2: So our advocacy is practical. Students meet local employers, local employers sit in on our reviews, and our results go to the people deciding on research funding and workforce programs.

Outreach (four)
01 Schools: Visits and hands-on demos for kids who've never seen a satellite up close.
02 Communities: Public talks and open events in Clemson and around the state.
03 Industry: Sponsorship, mentorship, and technical review from regional companies, plus an early look at the students they'll want to hire.
04 Policy: Conversations with state and local leaders about research funding, workforce programs, and what space work needs here.

Get involved
H2: Help us make the case.
- Host a visit at your school, library, or community group.
- Mentor or sponsor a student team or a specific outreach event.
- Introduce us to someone who should be part of the conversation.

## X-PLORE (Advocacy page section; short mentions on Home and in the contact form)

H2: X-PLORE: a semester as a spacecraft team
Intro: X-PLORE (Exploration Projects for Learning, Outreach, Research, and Engineering) gives a team of middle or high school students the job of a spacecraft engineering division for a full semester. It runs the way a real mission team works: choices get justified, designs get reviewed, and the work ends in front of people who do this for a living.
Steps (in order):
- Pick a payload: Each team chooses what its spacecraft will carry, then runs trade studies to back up every choice.
- Defend it every month: Teams meet Clemson CubeSat members once a month for reviews modeled on NASA's Preliminary Design Reviews.
- Write it up: They write a research proposal for an informed scientific audience, and can build a prototype if they want to.
- Take it home: Outreach events carry the project into their own schools and neighborhoods.
- Present at the Summit: The semester closes at the Clemson X-PLORE Summit, where teams present research posters to PhD researchers, NASA scientists, and industry engineers.
